Bibliography

Work on choosing what to compute and learn next, and on making the result of that choice believable.

arXiv identifiers were resolved against the arXiv API and journal DOIs against Crossref in September and October 2026. Venues are given where the arXiv record states them.

The core

  • Russell, S., and Wefald, E. (1991). Principles of Metareasoning. Artificial Intelligence 49(1–3), 361–395. Computations are actions valued by their effect on later actions. The rational agent performs the computation of highest net value and acts when none remains positive.
  • Hay, N., Russell, S., Tolpin, D., and Shimony, S. E. (2012). Selecting Computations: Theory and Applications. arXiv:1207.5879; UAI 2012. Metalevel control as a Bayesian selection problem rather than a bandit problem, since only the final choice collects reward. Finite sampling bounds in some cases, and a counterexample to the conjecture that an optimal policy always reaches a decision.
  • Callaway, F., Gul, S., Krueger, P. M., Griffiths, T. L., and Lieder, F. (2017). Learning to select computations. arXiv:1711.06892. Bayesian metalevel policy search. The value of information lies between the myopic value and the value of perfect information, and that bracket makes a learned metalevel policy tractable.
  • Turtel, B., Wilczewski, P., Franklin, D., and Skothiem, K. (2026). Future-as-Label: Scalable Supervision from Real-World Outcomes. arXiv:2601.06336. Resolved outcomes as reward. The predictor sees causally masked information, a resolver establishes the outcome, and a proper scoring rule scores the forecast. A 27% Brier improvement for Qwen3-32B. Offline, binary outcomes only. Deployment-time loops are not evaluated.
  • Li, Y., Yang, X., Yang, X., Xu, M., Wang, X., et al. (2025). R&D-Agent-Quant: A Multi-Agent Framework for Data-Centric Factors and Model Joint Optimization. arXiv:2505.15155; NeurIPS 2025. A full research loop over factors and models: research, development and feedback stages, with a multi-armed bandit choosing the next direction. Up to twice the annualised return of classical factor libraries with 70% fewer factors. Its limitations section names alternative data, domain knowledge and online adaptation as open.
  • Gençay, E. (2026). What survives honest evaluation? Leakage-safe, search-aware assessment of LLM-driven trading strategy discovery. arXiv:2608.27734. Leakage-safe tools by construction and deflation by the agent’s own trial count. Rejects every LLM-discovered strategy tested, and shows a leaky oracle with Sharpe 35 passing both the deflated Sharpe ratio and the backtest-overfitting test.
  • Russell, S. J. and Subramanian, D. (1995). Provably Bounded-Optimal Agents. arXiv:cs/9505103; Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 2, 575–609. Replaces perfect rationality with bounded optimality: an agent’s program should be the best one its architecture allows for its task environment. Constructs bounded-optimal agents for a class of real-time environments, illustrated with a mail-sorting model, and gives universal programs that are asymptotically bounded-optimal under any real-time constraints.

Budgeted races

  • Cotton, P. (2026). Wavefront Pruning in Budgeted Brownian Races. Working draft; software at microprediction/brownianbandit. A controller pays per unit time for every Brownian path kept alive and prunes to maximise the expected terminal maximum. A path is kept while its future pivotal value exceeds its carrying cost. The value of computation for parallel rollouts and agent fleets.
  • Cotton, P. (2026). Posterior-Predictive Pass-at-k. Note. Pass@k is the expected-maximum payoff of a best-of-k batch. A posterior expectation replaces the downward-biased plug-in and cuts held-out log loss from 3.37 to 0.48.
  • Cotton, P. (2026). When the Grass Is Greener: Three-Shot Search on Exponentiated Gaussian Landscapes. microprediction/browniansearch. The search-side sibling: where to spend a few evaluations of one path, paid at the final point.
  • Cotton, P. (2026). Clay Shooting: Equilibrium Effort Solving Outstanding Problems. Working draft. The strategic sibling. A Brownian attempt buys success probability at a convex price, and the record of solved problems implies what an attempt is worth to whoever makes it.

Pricing information and computation

  • Howard, R. A. (1966). Information Value Theory. IEEE Transactions on Systems Science and Cybernetics 2(1), 22–26. The value of information as the gain in expected utility from observing before deciding.
  • Matheson, J. E. (1968). The Economic Value of Analysis and Computation. IEEE Transactions on Systems Science and Cybernetics 4(3), 325–332. Extends the value of information from observations to analysis. The earliest statement of the value of computation.
  • Gittins, J. C. (1979). Bandit Processes and Dynamic Allocation Indices. Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, Series B 41(2), 148–164. Index policies for allocating effort among alternatives, optimal under the bandit-process assumptions.
  • Horvitz, E. J. (2013). Reasoning About Beliefs and Actions Under Computational Resource Constraints. arXiv:1304.2759; UAI 1987. Decision-theoretic control of inference under resource limits: weigh the costs and benefits of approximation procedures, and of acting on a partial result.
  • Hansen, E. A., and Zilberstein, S. (1996). Monitoring Anytime Algorithms. ACM SIGART Bulletin 7(2), 28–33. When to stop an algorithm that improves with time and can be interrupted.
  • Tolpin, D. and Shimony, S. E. (2012). MCTS Based on Simple Regret. arXiv:1207.5536; AAAI 2012. UCT minimises cumulative regret, but search needs simple regret. Optimising the sampling is itself a metareasoning problem.
  • Sezener, E. and Dayan, P. (2020). Static and Dynamic Values of Computation in MCTS. arXiv:2002.04335; UAI 2020. Values Monte Carlo simulations by their expected effect on the action eventually chosen, beyond the myopic horizon.
  • Weitzman, M. L. (1979). Optimal Search for the Best Alternative. Econometrica 47(3). The Pandora’s box problem: boxes are opened at a cost, each reveals a random reward, and the searcher keeps the best reward found. Weitzman’s rule is the optimal strategy when the reward distributions are known.
  • Luby, M., Sinclair, A., and Zuckerman, D. (1993). Optimal Speedup of Las Vegas Algorithms. Information Processing Letters 47(4), 173–180. A Las Vegas algorithm is always correct when it stops but has random running time, and is rerun with fresh randomness under a sequence of cutoffs. With the runtime distribution known, the best strategy repeats one fixed cutoff. Without it, a simple universal sequence has expected time O(ℓ. Log ℓ), where ℓ. Is the optimal expected time, and no universal strategy does better by more than a constant factor.
  • Zilberstein, S. (1995). Optimizing Decision Quality with Contract Algorithms. IJCAI-95, Vol. 2, pp. 1576–1581. Meta-level control of a composition of contract algorithms, whose run time must be fixed before they start. Each component is described by a probabilistic conditional performance profile that maps input quality and time to a distribution of output quality, and time is allocated by an offline compilation that simplifies run-time monitoring.
  • Poh, K.-L. and Horvitz, E. J. (1996). A Graph-Theoretic Analysis of Information Value. arXiv:1302.3596; UAI 1996. Dominance relations for the expected value of information on chance variables, read from their position in an influence diagram. These give non-numerical procedures for ordering uncertain variables by informational relevance.
  • Chen, C.-H., Lin, J., Yücesan, E., and Chick, S. E. (2000). Simulation Budget Allocation for Further Enhancing the Efficiency of Ordinal Optimization. Discrete Event Dynamic Systems 10(3), 251–270. Allocates simulation replications across designs to select the best at lower total cost. More efficient than the compared procedures, including a two-stage procedure, with a speedup factor above 20 beyond that of ordinal optimisation itself on a 210-design example.
  • Hansen, E. A., and Zilberstein, S. (2001). Monitoring and Control of Anytime Algorithms: A Dynamic Programming Approach. Artificial Intelligence 126(1–2), 139–157. When to stop an anytime algorithm and act, posed as a dynamic program. Handles uncertainty in the performance profile, uncertainty in the environment and the cost of monitoring progress, giving a non-myopic stopping policy.
  • Horvitz, E. (2001). Principles and Applications of Continual Computation. Artificial Intelligence 126(1–2), 159–196. Continual computation: spending idle time on problems that may arrive later, not only on those already posed. Policies for allocating idle time in several settings, with applications.
  • Kautz, H., Horvitz, E., Ruan, Y., Gomes, C., and Selman, B. (2002). Dynamic Restart Policies. AAAI 2002. Restart policies for randomized search that update beliefs about the runtime distribution from real-time observations of the instance and the solver, using learned Bayesian predictive models. On fresh test data, dynamic policies cut solution times by about 40% to 65% relative to the fixed optimal restart policy of Luby et al.
  • Finkelstein, L., Markovitch, S., and Rivlin, E. (2003). Optimal Schedules for Parallelizing Anytime Algorithms: The Case of Shared Resources. arXiv:1106.5269; Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 19, 73–138. Several anytime algorithm–problem pairs run on one shared processor until the first succeeds. An algorithm for the optimal schedule given the runtime distributions, analysed theoretically and empirically for several distribution types and tested on the Latin Square problem.
  • Glynn, P. and Juneja, S. (2004). A Large Deviations Perspective on Ordinal Optimization. Proceedings of the 2004 Winter Simulation Conference. Asymptotically optimal allocation of a sampling budget for selecting the population with the best mean, for general rather than Gaussian distributions. The large deviations rate function turns the allocation into a concave program, and at the optimum the probabilities of falsely selecting each competitor are equal on a logarithmic scale.
  • Giles, M. B. (2008). Multilevel Monte Carlo Path Simulation. Operations Research 56(3), 607–617. Multigrid ideas applied to Monte Carlo estimation of an expectation from a stochastic differential equation: cheap coarse paths and costly fine paths are combined in a telescoping sum. For a Lipschitz payoff with Euler discretisation, the cost to reach accuracy &epsilon. Falls from O(ε−3) to O(ε−2(log ε)2).
  • Xu, L., Hutter, F., Hoos, H. H., and Leyton-Brown, K. (2008). SATzilla: Portfolio-based Algorithm Selection for SAT. Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 32, 565–606. Chooses a SAT solver per instance: empirical hardness models predict each solver’s performance and the portfolio runs the predicted best. SATzilla07 won three gold, one silver and one bronze medal in the 2007 SAT Competition.
  • Krause, A. and Guestrin, C. (2009). Optimal Value of Information in Graphical Models. arXiv:1401.3474; Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 35, 557–591. Efficient optimal algorithms for selecting observations, and conditional observation plans, in chain graphical models such as hidden Markov models. On the other side, optimising value of information is NPPP-hard even for polytrees, and computing decision-theoretic value of information is #P-complete even for naive Bayes models.
  • Shahaf, D., and Horvitz, E. (2009). Investigations of Continual Computation. IJCAI 2009. Extends continual computation to shared subtasks and multiple tasks, with complexity results and approximations for arbitrary models of computational performance. Also treats uncertainty about which algorithm to apply, learning about performance, and the cost of delayed use of results.
  • Burns, E., Ruml, W., and Do, M. B. (2013). Heuristic Search When Time Matters. Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 47, 697–740. Heuristic search under a utility that trades search time linearly against solution cost. Bugsy puts the utility function into the search itself instead of relying on a separately trained termination policy for an anytime algorithm. Offline parameter tuning performed best when representative training instances were available, and Bugsy did well without any training.
  • Tolpin, D., Beja, T., Shimony, S. E., Felner, A., and Karpas, E. (2013). Towards Rational Deployment of Multiple Heuristics in A*. arXiv:1305.5030; IJCAI 2013. Lazy A* computes an expensive admissible heuristic only when it is needed for a decision, and rational lazy A* decides whether to compute it at all from a myopic value-of-information estimate. Both are analysed and evaluated on several search domains.
  • López-Ortiz, A., Angelopoulos, S., and Hamel, A. M. (2014). Optimal Scheduling of Contract Algorithms for Anytime Problem-Solving. Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 51, 533–554. Running a contract algorithm repeatedly with growing budgets makes it interruptible, at a cost measured by the acceleration ratio. Matching upper and lower bounds on that ratio for several problem instances on several parallel processors, resolving an open conjecture.
  • Giles, M. B. (2015). Multilevel Monte Carlo Methods. Acta Numerica 24, 259–328; arXiv:1304.5472. A review of multilevel Monte Carlo since the 2008 path-simulation paper, stressing the simplicity, flexibility and generality of the approach, with some original ideas and open directions.
  • Lin, C. H., Kolobov, A., Kamar, E., and Horvitz, E. (2015). Metareasoning for Planning Under Uncertainty. arXiv:1505.00399; IJCAI 2015, pp. 1601–1609. Online planning in an MDP when the state keeps changing while the agent thinks. General metareasoning is shown to be at most polynomially harder than solving the MDP, but optimal metareasoning is impractical, so the paper gives approximate procedures built on properties of the BRTDP planner.
  • Agarwal, A., Luo, H., Neyshabur, B., and Schapire, R. E. (2017). Corralling a Band of Bandit Algorithms. arXiv:1612.06246; COLT 2017. A master algorithm over several bandit algorithms that aims to do almost as well as the best of them would do if run alone. Base algorithms inside a master receive less feedback, so the master must not starve one that starts poorly but would improve. A version of online mirror descent with a special mirror map and learning-rate schedule gives better regret bounds than earlier combiners.
  • Karpas, E., Betzalel, O., Shimony, S. E., Tolpin, D., and Felner, A. (2018). Rational Deployment of Multiple Heuristics in Optimal State-Space Search. Artificial Intelligence 256, 181–210. The journal extension, adding lazy and rational lazy IDA* and methods for estimating the parameters the decision rule needs. The rational variants beat their non-rational lazy counterparts in several domains.
  • Singla, S. (2018). The Price of Information in Combinatorial Optimization. arXiv:1711.00405; SODA 2018, 2523–2532. Extends Weitzman’s Pandora’s box, where values are revealed only by paying a price, to combinatorial problems such as spanning tree, matching, set cover, facility location and prize-collecting Steiner tree. A reduction to the versions of these problems without prices gives exact or approximation algorithms for expected utility or disutility.
  • Binois, M., Huang, J., Gramacy, R. B., and Ludkovski, M. (2019). Replication or Exploration? Sequential Design for Stochastic Simulation Experiments. arXiv:1710.03206; Technometrics 61(1), 7–23. Shows that replication helps Gaussian process emulation of noisy simulators, both statistically and computationally. A lookahead sequential design decides whether the next run repeats an existing input or explores a new one and, paired with a heteroskedastic Gaussian process, learns how signal and noise vary across the input space. Demonstrated on inventory management and epidemiology simulations.
  • Gupta, A., Jiang, H., Scully, Z., and Singla, S. (2019). The Markovian Price of Information. arXiv:1902.07856; IPCO 2019, LNCS 11480, 233–246. Each input of a combinatorial problem is the destination of a Markov chain that costs a price per step to advance, and rewards can be collected only for a subset meeting a constraint. Gives optimal and approximation algorithms that trade the value of the combinatorial solution against the total price paid, joining Markovian multi-armed bandits with discrete optimization, and treats robustness to the distribution parameters and commitment constraints.
  • Jackson, C., Presanis, A., Conti, S., and De Angelis, D. (2019). Value of Information: Sensitivity Analysis and Research Design in Bayesian Evidence Synthesis. arXiv:1703.08994; Journal of the American Statistical Association 114(528), 1436–1449. Value-of-information analysis for a Bayesian model combining several evidence sources: which parameter uncertainties drive decision uncertainty, and the expected reduction in loss from collecting data of a given design. Applied to estimating HIV prevalence from surveys, registers and expert beliefs.
  • Kleinberg, R., Leyton-Brown, K., Lucier, B., and Graham, D. (2019). Procrastinating with Confidence: Near-Optimal, Anytime, Adaptive Algorithm Configuration. arXiv:1902.05454; NeurIPS 2019. Algorithm configuration with runtime caps. Structured Procrastination with Confidence keeps the near-optimality and anytime guarantees of Structured Procrastination while adapting to the problem, so it runs much faster when many configurations are poor. Experiments show such settings are common and that the anytime property helps find good configurations quickly.
  • Shperberg, S. S., Coles, A., Karpas, E., Shimony, S. E., and Ruml, W. (2020). Trading Plan Cost for Timeliness in Situated Temporal Planning. IJCAI 2020, 4176–4182. Extends deliberation scheduling for situated temporal planning, where a plan can become infeasible while the agent is still planning, to plans whose costs differ and are uncertain until found. Minimising expected cost is PSPACE-hard and, even with known costs and deadlines, the optimal schedule is contingent rather than sequential. A greedy scheme built on special cases performs well, including on problems generated from planner search trees.
  • Heath, A., Strong, M., Glynn, D., Kunst, N., Welton, N. J., and Goldhaber-Fiebert, J. D. (2022). Simulating Study Data to Support Expected Value of Sample Information Calculations: A Tutorial. Medical Decision Making 42(2), 143–155. A step-by-step guide to simulating data from proposed studies for expected value of sample information calculations, including correlated outcomes, missingness, censoring and reuse of individual patient data, with R code.
  • Angelopoulos, S., and Kamali, S. (2023). Contract Scheduling with Predictions. arXiv:2011.12439; Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 77, 395–426. Contract scheduling with a possibly erroneous prediction of the interruption time, given either directly or as answers to binary queries. Tradeoffs between robustness to adversarial predictions and consistency when the prediction is correct.
  • Elboher, A., Bensoussan, A., Karpas, E., Ruml, W., Shperberg, S. S., and Shimony, S. E. (2023). A Formal Metareasoning Model of Concurrent Planning and Execution. arXiv:2303.02664; AAAI 2023. Formalises concurrent planning and execution as an abstract metareasoning problem: acting before search concludes buys search time but risks wrong choices, especially when actions are irreversible. The general problem is intractable, some special cases are solvable in polynomial time, and greedy algorithms perform well on instances derived from search problems.
  • Angelopoulos, S., Bienkowski, M., Dürr, C., and Simon, B. (2024). Contract Scheduling with Distributional and Multiple Advice. arXiv:2404.12485; IJCAI 2024, 3652–3660. Contract scheduling when the prediction of the interruption time is a probability distribution or a set of possible times. Schedules that are optimal when the prediction is accurate while keeping the best worst-case performance when it is adversarial.
  • Coles, A., Karpas, E., Lavrinenko, A., Ruml, W., Shimony, S. E., and Shperberg, S. S. (2024). Planning and Acting While the Clock Ticks. arXiv:2403.14796; ICAPS 2024. Introduces concurrent planning and execution, where actions may be dispatched before planning terminates and wall-clock deadlines affect action applicability and goal achievement. Extends metareasoning for situated temporal planning to this setting, and under strong time pressure the approach outperforms situated temporal planning.
  • Jaimungal, S. and Shi, X. (2024). The Price of Information. arXiv:2402.11864; SIAM Journal on Financial Mathematics 15(3), SC54–SC67. The indifference price an expected-utility investor would pay for partial information about an asset’s trajectory, comparing the partial-information control problem without the purchase to the problem after it. When the information can be bought at any stopping time before the horizon, the investor buys at a deterministic time.
  • Bansal, N., Huang, Z., and Zhu, Z. (2025). Optimal 4-Approximation for the Correlated Pandora’s Problem. arXiv:2509.17029; FOCS 2025. Pandora’s box with correlated box values, a problem that also generalizes min-sum set cover. Gives a 4-approximation, matching the lower bound of 4 inherited from min-sum set cover.
  • Cao, Z., Wang, H., Chew, E. P., Li, H., and Tan, K. C. (2025). A Budget-Adaptive Allocation Rule for Optimal Computing Budget Allocation. arXiv:2304.02377; European Journal of Operational Research 325(2), 247–260. The asymptotic OCBA rule follows from assuming a very large budget and so ignores the budget’s size. A budget-adaptive rule in which the share of budget given to each design depends on the total budget, with two heuristic algorithms built on it.
  • Coles, A., Karpas, E., Shimony, S. E., Shperberg, S. S., and Ruml, W. (2025). Concurrent Planning and Execution Using Dispatch-Dependent Values. IJCAI 2025, 8483–8490. Earlier methods for concurrent planning and execution used a value function that assumes search finishes before any action is executed. A new value function accounts for dispatching actions incrementally, yields a simpler algorithm, and under time pressure significantly outperforms the previous state of the art.
  • Li, Z., Wan, Y., and Hong, L. J. (2025). Sequential Additivity in Distributionally Robust Ranking and Selection. arXiv:2509.06147. Ranking and selection under input uncertainty picks the alternative with the best worst-case mean over several input distributions, giving a multiplicative number of scenarios. Any consistent procedure must sample an additive number of scenarios infinitely often, and a simple additive allocation attains this bound with exponentially decaying probability of incorrect selection. The scenarios sampled infinitely often need not be the true worst cases.
  • Margraf, V., Koerner, T., Tornede, A., and Wever, M. (2025). RunAndSchedule2Survive: Algorithm Scheduling Based on Run2Survive. ACM Transactions on Evolutionary Learning and Optimization 5(3). Algorithm scheduling from runtime distributions modelled as survival functions, with an evolutionary algorithm searching over schedules. Best performance in 20 of 25 benchmark scenarios against earlier approaches.
  • Nadimpalli, S., Qiao, M., and Rubinfeld, R. (2025). No Price Tags? No Problem: Query Strategies for Unpriced Information. arXiv:2511.06170. In the priced query model a known function is computed on an unknown input whose variables are revealed only at a cost, and earlier work assumes the costs are known in advance. Lets the costs be unknown, proves that this imposes an unavoidable overhead on every query strategy, and gives strategies that essentially match the lower bound and are competitive with the best cost-aware strategies for arbitrary Boolean functions.
  • Wang, Y. and Zhou, E. (2025). Data-Driven Ranking and Selection With Simultaneous Input Data Collection and Simulation. arXiv:2503.11773; Naval Research Logistics 72(8), 1170–1183. Ranking and selection when input data arrive in streams that can be collected while simulations run. Simulation outputs under changing input distributions are pooled into one performance estimator, and a multi-stage procedure divides the budget between collecting input data and simulating, with consistency and asymptotic normality guarantees and numerical studies.
  • Labiosa, A., and Hanna, J. P. (2026). When to Plan: Learning to Select Between Reactive Control and Deliberative Planning. arXiv:2607.16421; RLC 2026. Trains with reinforcement learning a meta-reasoning policy that chooses between a reactive policy and slower decision-time planning, conditioning on an uncertainty score for the reactive policy. In motion-planning and navigation environments it learns when the reactive action is good enough, and shifts toward fully reactive control as the reactive policy improves.
  • Labiosa, A., and Hanna, J. P. (2026). Learning to Act While Thinking. RL in Big Worlds Workshop, RLC 2026. Formulates the Real-Time Meta-Reasoning MDP, in which an agent may request a plan at any time but the plan is costly and arrives after a delay while the environment keeps changing. A curriculum-based reinforcement learning method learns when to invoke the planner, how to act while waiting and how to follow the plan, and in two partially observable dynamic tasks it reaches a higher success rate with fewer plans than fixed meta-reasoning and heuristic baselines.
  • Lyu, Z., Nejma, C., Wegel, T., Yang, F., and Dorner, F. E. (2026). Cutting LLM Evaluation Costs with SySRs: A Bandit Algorithm That Provably Exploits Model Similarity. arXiv:2606.07726; ICML 2026, PMLR 306. Successive Rejects run on paired comparisons: every surviving model is scored on the same new queries and one is eliminated per phase. Hyperparameter-free, with error guarantees that improve as the evaluated models become more similar. Lowest average error rate among the compared methods across 15 standard LLM benchmarks.
  • Tuisov, A. (2026). Search as Computation Allocation. arXiv:2607.27871. Treats search as allocating costly computations that matter only through terminal decision loss, with Bellman equations for fixed budgets, priced computation and certification. Mutual information equals myopic value of computation under log loss, but under simple regret information gain can rank computations arbitrarily poorly, and weighted A* is recovered under an explicit heuristic-error model. Theory with illustrative instantiations, and no experiments.
  • Xue, B., Cheng, J., Lyu, S.-H., Wan, Y., and Qiu, S. (2026). Test-Time Scaling via Budgeted Multi-Attribute Verification. arXiv:2609.34322. Verification of LLM answers under a shared budget, posed as multi-attribute good-arm identification: each candidate is scored on several costly attributes and is certified only if every attribute mean clears its threshold. BMA-GAI chooses which candidate and which attribute to sample next, certifies with anytime-valid bounds, and has a matching information-theoretic converse, so it is first-order optimal away from critical budget levels.

Resource-rational metareasoning

  • Lieder, F., and Griffiths, T. L. (2017). Strategy Selection as Rational Metareasoning. Psychological Review 124(6), 762–794. Human strategy choice modelled as learned metareasoning.
  • Lieder, F., Shenhav, A., Musslick, S., and Griffiths, T. L. (2018). Rational Metareasoning and the Plasticity of Cognitive Control. PLOS Computational Biology 14(4), e1006043. Cognitive control adapted by learning which computations are worth their cost.
  • Lieder, F., and Griffiths, T. L. (2020). Resource-Rational Analysis: Understanding Human Cognition as the Optimal Use of Limited Computational Resources. Behavioral and Brain Sciences 43. The research programme, with open peer commentary.
  • Milli, S., Lieder, F., and Griffiths, T. L. (2017). When Does Bounded-Optimal Metareasoning Favor Few Cognitive Systems?. Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence 31(1). Restricts metareasoning to a choice among a few cognitive systems that do different amounts of computation, and asks how many a bounded-optimal agent should have. In a two-alternative decision and in planning in a Markov decision process, the best number depends on the variability of the environment and the cost of metareasoning. When two systems are optimal, one is fast and error-prone and the other slow and accurate.
  • Sezener, C. E., Dezfouli, A., and Keramati, M. (2019). Optimizing the depth and the direction of prospective planning using information values. PLOS Computational Biology 15(3), e1006827. In plan-until-habit, a search tree is simulated to limited depth and habitual values stand in for what lies beyond. Chooses the direction and depth of expansion by a speed/accuracy tradeoff based on information values, shown in a grid world. The model accounts for the effect of time pressure on planning depth, of reward magnitude on planning direction, and the shift from goal-directed to habitual behavior with training.
  • Adriaensen, S., Biedenkapp, A., Shala, G., et al. (2022). Automated Dynamic Algorithm Configuration. arXiv:2205.13881; Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 75, 1633–1699. Learning policies that adjust an algorithm’s parameters while it runs, rather than fixing a configuration in advance. Formalises dynamic algorithm configuration as a computational problem, surveys the methods used for it, and reports case studies in evolutionary optimisation, AI planning and machine learning.
  • Godara, P., and Alemán, T. D. (2024). Metareasoning in uncertain environments: a meta-BAMDP framework. arXiv:2408.01253. Extends models of metareasoning to environments with unknown rewards and transitions by embedding the reasoning problem in a Bayes-adaptive MDP. Applied to Bernoulli bandit tasks, with two theorems that make approximate solutions tractable and give testable predictions about human exploration under cognitive limits.
  • Russek, E. M., Acosta-Kane, D., van Opheusden, B., Mattar, M. G., and Griffiths, T. L. (2025). Time Spent Thinking in Online Chess Reflects the Value of Computation. Cognitive Science 49(10), e70119. Compares thinking time in over 12 million online chess games with the value of computation in each position. Players think longer where more computation is more beneficial, the relation is stronger in stronger players, and a model built on the measured cost of time captures its qualitative features.

Information acquisition

  • Frazier, P. I., Powell, W. B., and Dayanik, S. (2008). A Knowledge-Gradient Policy for Sequential Information Collection. SIAM Journal on Control and Optimization 47(5), 2410–2439. The one-step expected improvement in the value of the best alternative.
  • Russo, D. and Van Roy, B. (2014). Learning to Optimize via Information-Directed Sampling. arXiv:1403.5556. Acts to minimise squared expected regret per unit of information about the optimal action. A regret bound that scales with the entropy of the optimal action.
  • Frazier, P. I. (2018). A Tutorial on Bayesian Optimization. arXiv:1807.02811. Expected improvement, entropy search and the knowledge gradient, with multi-fidelity and multi-source variants.
  • Li, L., Jamieson, K., DeSalvo, G., Rostamizadeh, A., and Talwalkar, A. (2016). Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization. arXiv:1603.06560. Adaptive resource allocation with early stopping over random configurations, reported an order of magnitude faster than Bayesian optimisation on its benchmarks. The non-Bayesian counterpoint.
  • Li, Y. and Oliva, J. B. (2020). Active Feature Acquisition with Generative Surrogate Models. arXiv:2010.02433. Which missing features to pay for at prediction time, with a generative surrogate estimating the information gain of each.
  • Wan, B., Liu, M., Grigas, P., and Shen, Z.-J. M. (2026). Decision-Focused Sequential Experimental Design: A Directional Uncertainty-Guided Approach. arXiv:2602.05340. Sequential experimental design aimed at decision loss rather than prediction error. Stops earlier than decision-blind designs.
  • Maron, O., and Moore, A. W. (1994). Hoeffding Races: Accelerating Model Selection Search for Classification and Function Approximation.
